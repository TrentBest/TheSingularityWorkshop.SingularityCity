# Law Enforcement & Public Safety

*protection, authority and public trust*

**Sourcebook ID:** `singularity-city.domain.law-enforcement-and-public-safety`  
**Edition:** `0.1.0`  

**Owner:** Singularity City · **LOD:** 2 institutional specialization · **Status:** authored domain sourcebook; not an operational policing or emergency-dispatch system.

A city needs ways to prevent harm, respond when harm occurs, protect people in danger, and maintain public spaces where people can live together. Those responsibilities require authority—but authority must be bounded, visible, reviewable and answerable to the people affected by it.

This book models law enforcement and public safety as civic institutions, not as a single guard character or an omnipotent city controller. It applies to a village watch, town constabulary, city police service, imperial patrol, ranger corps, magical peacekeepers, or future public-safety agency. The form changes with the setting; the need to distinguish service, authority, evidence and accountability does not.

## 1. The public-safety system

Public safety is broader than policing. A city may include:
- emergency medical response and public health;
- fire, rescue and disaster response;
- safeguarding and protection services;
- traffic and transport safety;
- environmental and building safety inspectors;
- peace officers, constables, watch, patrol and investigators;
- community mediators, wardens and restorative services;
- courts, prosecutors, defense services and oversight bodies.

These services have different purposes, qualifications, powers and limits. A medic is not a police officer; a fire marshal is not automatically a criminal investigator; a neighborhood volunteer does not acquire coercive authority by wearing a badge.

## 2. Institutional identity and jurisdiction

Represent each agency with a stable identity and an explicit charter:
- purpose and public mandate;
- geographic, subject-matter and temporal jurisdiction;
- enabling law, charter or recognized custom;
- powers granted and powers expressly withheld;
- leadership, staffing, qualifications and duty assignments;
- operating resources, facilities and service coverage;
- reporting lines and independent oversight;
- complaint, review and appeal routes;
- records-retention and public-disclosure rules.

Jurisdiction is not simply a map boundary. It can depend on the type of incident, the people involved, the place, the time, treaty or customary arrangements, and overlapping authorities. A city guard may patrol city streets but lack authority inside an embassy, private estate, temple sanctuary or neighboring jurisdiction.

A character's job title, uniform, faction membership or reputation does not prove that a particular action is authorized. Model appointment, assignment, jurisdiction, legal authority and digital authorization as separate facts.

## 3. Public-safety roles

| Role | Primary responsibility | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Patrol officer / watch member | Visible presence, assistance, initial response and observation | Does not decide guilt |
| Investigator | Establish facts and preserve evidence | Findings remain reviewable |
| Dispatcher | Receive requests and coordinate resources | Dispatch is not a finding of fault |
| Medic / healer | Stabilize and treat people | Clinical care is not interrogation |
| Fire / rescue crew | Protect life and address hazards | Rescue priorities differ from punishment |
| Inspector | Assess compliance within a defined remit | Inspection finding is not automatically a criminal conviction |
| Mediator | Help parties reach voluntary resolution | Cannot compel agreement |
| Supervisor | Allocate staff and review decisions | Cannot erase independent oversight |
| Independent reviewer | Examine complaints, patterns and conduct | Must have access and independence sufficient for the task |

A single character may hold several roles in a small settlement, but conflicts of interest and procedural safeguards should remain visible. The reeve who investigates a dispute and later judges it is a different institutional arrangement from a system with independent adjudication.

## 4. Incidents, observations and evidence

An incident record should preserve:
- stable incident identity, time and location;
- reporting source and how the report was received;
- affected people, property and public services;
- the distinction between observed facts, reported claims and interpretation;
- response actions and the authority claimed for each;
- evidence items, custody history, provenance and known gaps;
- injuries, losses, disruption and immediate protective needs;
- referrals, follow-up, review and unresolved questions.

A call or allegation is not proof. A witness statement is not the same as a verified observation. A confession may be contested. Evidence can be incomplete, mistaken, contaminated, fabricated or lawfully excluded. Preserve original records and corrections rather than silently rewriting history.

Use explicit attribution: **reported by**, **observed by**, **recorded by**, **inferred by**, **disputed by**, **found by an authorized body**. A system must not turn an unverified suspicion into a permanent resident attribute.

## 5. Response, necessity and proportionality

A response should be explainable in terms of the danger known at the time, the lawful mandate, the options available, the urgency, the likely consequences and the people affected. Do not assume that force is the default response to noncompliance, poverty, homelessness, protest, unusual behavior or a disputed accusation.

Scenario authors should make visible:
- the immediate safety objective;
- the legal or customary basis for action;
- the least harmful feasible options considered;
- urgency and uncertainty;
- people at risk, including bystanders;
- accommodations, communication and de-escalation needs;
- action taken, duration and outcome;
- post-incident medical care, reporting and review.

The model is not a tactical manual. It is a way to represent choices, consequences, constraints and accountability in fictional settings.

## 6. Rights, safeguards and public trust

Rights and safeguards vary across legal traditions and fictional settings. A sourcebook should state the setting's rules rather than quietly assume a modern constitution—or quietly remove protections because the setting is medieval or fantastical.

Relevant questions include notice of authority, lawful grounds, privacy, search and seizure limits, detention review, access to counsel or advocate, protection from coercion, treatment of minors and vulnerable people, language access, disability accommodation, religious or cultural protections, and remedies for abuse.

Measure institutional legitimacy through relationships and evidence, not a single “trust” number. Residents may trust one service and fear another; experiences may differ by district, status, history and individual. A low complaint count can mean good service—or barriers to reporting.

## 7. Complaints, oversight and learning

Accountability needs more than an internal supervisor. Possible mechanisms include:
- civilian or community oversight;
- independent inspectorates;
- judicial review;
- elected-body hearings;
- ombuds offices;
- professional standards bodies;
- public reporting and audit;
- protected whistleblowing;
- remedy, compensation and institutional reform.

Record a complaint independently of whether it is substantiated. Preserve the complainant's account, the agency response, evidence, reviewer, finding, remedy and any appeal. A complaint being closed is not the same as the affected person receiving justice.

## 8. Emergency management and resilience

Public safety also includes floods, fires, storms, disease, infrastructure failures, industrial accidents, magical disasters and conflict. Model hazard, exposure, vulnerability, service dependencies, warning, evacuation, shelter, triage, response capacity, recovery and lessons learned.

A city should expose who receives warnings, who can move, which routes are accessible, where shelters exist, what services fail together, and how recovery resources are allocated. Physics owns physical hazard and propagation models; City owns affected places, institutions, populations, access and civic response relationships.

## 9. Encounters and campaign situations

- A respected watch captain is ordered to enforce a law the community considers unjust.
- An investigator's evidence clears a suspect but implicates a powerful officeholder.
- A flood overwhelms the official response and neighborhood volunteers become essential.
- Two jurisdictions dispute who may investigate an incident at their shared border.
- A patrol member uses authority outside their assigned remit; witnesses disagree about what happened.
- A city publishes reassuring safety statistics while residents cannot safely file complaints.
- A public-safety agency must rebuild trust after a historical abuse.
- A small village has one reeve performing several incompatible institutional roles.

## 11. Implementation boundary and validation

This is an authored sourcebook and domain contract, not a live dispatch, police, evidence-management, emergency-response or surveillance platform. It does not grant real-world authority or authorize real enforcement actions.

Validate with:
1. a village watch with overlapping civic roles;
2. a metropolitan agency with separate patrol, investigation and oversight;
3. an emergency crossing municipal boundaries;
4. a disputed allegation that is later corrected;
5. a complaint against a senior official;
6. a public protest where safety, expression and political disagreement remain distinct;
7. an emergency in which residents with different mobility and communication needs receive meaningful access to assistance.

The same people, institutions, incidents, places and evidence references must persist across the response, justice and governance books.
