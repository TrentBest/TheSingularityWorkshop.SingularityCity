# Politics, Power & Public Will

*influence, legitimacy and change*

**Sourcebook ID:** `singularity-city.domain.politics-power-and-public-will`  
**Edition:** `0.1.0`  

**Owner:** Singularity City · **LOD:** 2 civic specialization · **Status:** authored domain sourcebook; not a voter-profiling or political-persuasion system.

Politics is how people and groups contest, negotiate and exercise power over shared decisions. It includes elections, but also councils, assemblies, parties, factions, patronage, public movements, negotiation, protest, diplomacy, propaganda, resistance and succession. A city is political even when its rulers claim that politics has been abolished.

This book is for fictional worldbuilding, historical analysis and civic systems education. It does not prescribe a preferred ideology or reduce residents to demographic targets. Political belief, participation and affiliation are contextual, changeable and sometimes private.

Election mechanics and direct player participation are detailed in [Elections & Player Participation — LOD 2](elections-and-player-participation.md). That specialization defines candidate communications, including the deliberately ambient and sometimes repetitive background campaign advertisements, player voting, ballot privacy, campaign finance and result disputes.

## 1. Power has several sources

Power may arise from:
- lawful office and recognized authority;
- wealth, ownership, credit and control of scarce resources;
- expertise, information and institutional knowledge;
- social trust, reputation and community service;
- organization, numbers and collective action;
- coercion, military force or control of enforcement;
- religious, cultural or customary legitimacy;
- control of infrastructure, routes, communications or supply;
- external alliances and diplomatic recognition.

These sources can reinforce or contradict one another. A lawful official may lack public confidence. A popular movement may have influence but no legal mandate. A wealthy patron may dominate policy without holding office. Keep authority, influence, legitimacy and coercive capacity as separate relationships.

## 2. Political actors and organizations

Possible actors include:
- individual candidates and officeholders;
- parties, coalitions and parliamentary groups;
- neighborhood associations and civic movements;
- labor, trade and professional organizations;
- cultural, religious and customary authorities;
- advocacy groups and public-interest organizations;
- business associations and wealthy patrons;
- dissident, revolutionary and separatist movements;
- military, security and administrative elites;
- foreign states, city alliances and diplomatic missions.

Represent each actor through purpose, membership, public platform, internal factions, resources, supporters, opponents, relationships, decision process, public claims and historical actions. Do not assume that a party is ideologically uniform or that a member agrees with every platform statement.

## 3. Elections and representation

An electoral system needs explicit rules:
- who is eligible to participate and under what law;
- constituencies, districts or at-large representation;
- nomination and candidate access;
- ballot, ranking, delegation or other voting method;
- counting, thresholds and tie resolution;
- term length, vacancies and succession;
- recount, challenge and certification;
- accessibility, secrecy and protection from coercion.

Different systems produce different incentives and representation. First-past-the-post, proportional representation, ranked choice, sortition, councils of delegates and consensus assemblies should not be treated as interchangeable. The book should describe the chosen mechanism and its consequences, not claim one universal model.

A vote count is a record of a procedure, not proof that the process was free or fair. Preserve eligibility rules, turnout denominator, rejected ballots, observers, challenges, certification and known limitations. Do not infer an individual's political beliefs from a vote or use the model to target real voters.

## 4. Public opinion and collective action

Public will is not a single citywide number. People may disagree, abstain, change their minds, distrust all available choices or prioritize local concerns over national ones. Public opinion can be expressed through elections, petitions, public meetings, protests, strikes, boycotts, community assemblies, art, media, litigation or quiet non-cooperation.

For a fictional scenario, distinguish:
- individual belief and private preference;
- public statement and organized platform;
- survey or poll with method and sample limitations;
- turnout and participation;
- protest attendance estimate and source;
- organized movement membership;
- institutional response;
- observed policy outcome.

Do not treat a poll as a mandate or a protest crowd as a representative sample. Political data should be aggregated and purpose-limited; private affiliation and belief should not become a resident's permanent city profile.

## 5. Factions, coalitions and negotiation

A faction is a group organized around shared interests, leadership, identity or strategy. Coalitions form when actors cooperate despite disagreement. Model:
- shared goals and unresolved differences;
- bargaining positions and red lines;
- support, opposition and abstention;
- leadership and internal accountability;
- public commitments and private negotiations;
- resource contributions and dependency;
- confidence, defections and succession.

A coalition may be stable on one issue and divided on another. Political relationships are time-bound; today's ally may be tomorrow's opponent. Avoid a single alignment score that hides issue-specific relationships.

## 6. Campaigns, patronage and political integrity

Campaigns communicate proposals, records, values and criticism. Political integrity concerns include conflicts of interest, opaque funding, patronage, bribery, intimidation, misinformation, unequal media access, misuse of public resources and suppression of participation.

Represent claims and evidence separately. A rumor about a candidate is not a verified fact. A donation is not proof of a quid pro quo. A policy disagreement is not evidence of corruption. Record funding sources, disclosures, investigations and findings according to the setting's rules.

The book does not provide real-world persuasion optimization, demographic microtargeting or manipulation strategies. For tabletop play, political intrigue should focus on fictional motives, competing obligations, institutional consequences and the agency of the people involved.

## 7. Protest, dissent and resistance

A city should represent peaceful assembly, petition, labor action, civil disobedience, cultural resistance, underground publication and other forms of dissent. A regime may protect, restrict, tolerate, co-opt or violently suppress them. The sourcebook describes those choices and their consequences without equating dissent with disorder.

Track the stated demands, participants and represented constituencies, methods at a high level, institutional response, public impact, rights claims, injuries or arrests, independent reporting and later review. Do not infer that every participant shares one motive or that every protest is peaceful or violent.

## 8. Legitimacy, succession and regime change

Legitimacy can be claimed through election, inheritance, conquest, religious sanction, customary recognition, performance, treaty, revolution or external recognition. These sources may conflict. A ruler can be legally installed but widely rejected; a movement can be popular but divided about its future.

Succession and transfer scenarios include election, resignation, inheritance, appointment, coalition agreement, impeachment, abdication, negotiated transition, coup, occupation, revolution, secession and dissolution. Describe the mechanism, institutional continuity, violence or nonviolence, recognition and affected populations. Do not reduce a coup or revolution to a simple change of leader: services, courts, security forces, records, debts and public obligations may continue or fracture.

## 9. Media, information and political memory

Political communication shapes what residents know and believe. Model publishers, public broadcasters, independent journalists, rumor networks, censorship, archives, propaganda and information access. Preserve source, time, audience, corrections, ownership and uncertainty.

Public records should distinguish policy promises, adopted decisions, actual implementation and measured outcomes. Political actors may rewrite their own narratives; the city's event history should retain prior statements and subsequent corrections.

## 10. Encounters and campaign situations

- A coalition wins office but cannot agree on the budget that would fulfill its platform.
- A popular mayor loses an election and must transfer authority peacefully.
- A district petitions for autonomy while neighboring districts fear loss of shared services.
- A protest movement includes factions with different goals and tactics.
- A public poll contradicts the election result because the two measured different populations and questions.
- A wealthy patron funds civic improvements while seeking private influence over permits.
- A succession crisis divides the courts, military and civil service.
- A government calls critics disloyal; residents debate whether the law protects dissent.

## 12. Implementation boundary and validation

This is an authored worldbuilding and civic-systems sourcebook, not an election platform, voter database, political persuasion engine or predictor of real political behavior.

Validate with:
1. a small village election and a customary council;
2. a proportional city council with coalition formation;
3. a protest and a government response that are recorded separately;
4. a peaceful transfer of executive authority;
5. a succession crisis with conflicting legitimacy claims;
6. a public poll whose limitations are visible;
7. a patronage allegation that remains distinct from an adjudicated finding.
