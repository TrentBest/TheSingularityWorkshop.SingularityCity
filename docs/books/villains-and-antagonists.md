# Villains & Antagonists — opposition with agency and consequence

**Owner:** Singularity City · **LOD:** 2 character / narrative specialization · **Status:** authored sourcebook; not a morality classifier or real-world harm instruction.

A villain is not simply an enemy with a large health bar. A compelling antagonist has motives, capabilities, limits, relationships, resources, history, beliefs and choices. Their goals conflict with a protagonist, community or institution in ways that produce meaningful consequences. Some are cruel; some are convinced they are necessary; some are ordinary people making escalating compromises. A villain may be an individual, organization, institution, movement, machine intelligence or force where the setting supports it.

The word *villain* is often a narrative judgment. The world model should preserve what happened, who was harmed, what the actor intended, what they claim, what can be proven and how different observers interpret the events.

## 1. Antagonist is a relationship, not an ontology of evil

Distinguish:
- **Antagonist:** opposes a character's or group's immediate goal.
- **Adversary:** competes or conflicts with another actor; may act lawfully.
- **Rival:** recurring competitor with overlapping aims.
- **Villain:** an antagonist framed as causing serious harm or pursuing destructive ends.
- **Mastermind:** coordinates a plan or organization; not necessarily omniscient.
- **Tyrant:** exercises coercive rule without adequate accountability.
- **Predator:** exploits people or dependencies for advantage.
- **Reformer / revolutionary:** seeks structural change; may be ally or antagonist depending on goals and methods.
- **Tragic antagonist:** causes harm under pressure, belief, grief or constrained choices without being absolved of consequences.

A protagonist to one faction may be a villain to another. Record perspective and evidence instead of declaring every opposing actor objectively evil.

## 2. Character construction

Build an antagonist from the same LOD 1 character model as every other person:
- identity, aliases and continuity;
- goals, motives, fears and values;
- knowledge, beliefs, misconceptions and secrets;
- skills, resources, constraints and dependencies;
- relationships, loyalties, rivals and obligations;
- history, turning points and formative events;
- public reputation, private self-understanding and disputed accounts;
- boundaries, choices, consequences and capacity for change.

Do not reduce a character to a single alignment score, diagnosis, species, culture or appearance. Capabilities must have limits; plans can fail; information can be wrong; allies can disagree; institutions can resist.

## 3. Organization and scale

Antagonism can operate at several scales:
- one individual in a personal conflict;
- a small crew or household;
- a guild, corporation, cult, faction or criminal network;
- a public office or institution acting abusively;
- a state, empire, occupying force or political movement;
- a distributed system or nonhuman intelligence.

Organization does not imply perfect coordination. Members have distinct motives, access, authority and knowledge. A leader's intent is not automatically shared by every member. Dissent, defection, internal oversight, succession and fragmentation create story possibilities.

## 4. Villain plans as causal story structures

A plan can be represented through objective, motivation, assumptions, resources, dependencies, milestones, opposition, exposure risks, contingency, time horizon and possible failure states. Keep the plan at the level needed for narrative design and simulation. Do not turn this sourcebook into operational instructions for real-world crime, abuse, sabotage, weapons or evasion.

Stories become stronger when the antagonist's plan changes the world in visible ways: shortages, distrust, altered routes, damaged institutions, displaced residents, political conflict, environmental change or new alliances. Those consequences belong to their respective domain owners.

## 5. Player agency and moral complexity

Players may confront, negotiate with, expose, outmaneuver, forgive, oppose, cooperate with or become an antagonist. A villain may surrender, reform, escape, be acquitted, be convicted, be misunderstood or remain unresolved. Outcomes depend on evidence, choices, institutional rules and the setting—not a mandatory boss-fight ending.

Do not force a single moral answer. Make harms visible, give affected characters agency, and allow consequences to persist after the antagonist leaves the scene. Redemption is possible but does not erase victims' experiences or automatically restore trust.

## 6. Reputation, propaganda and public narrative

Public reputation can differ among neighborhoods, institutions, factions and individuals. Propaganda may frame an antagonist as a hero, traitor, monster or scapegoat. Preserve who made each claim, when, for what audience and with what evidence. Publicity is not proof; silence is not guilt; popularity is not legitimacy.

## 8. Validation scenarios

- A local antagonist has a comprehensible motive and limited knowledge.
- A respected public institution becomes the antagonist through a specific abusive policy.
- A villain's followers disagree about methods and objectives.
- A public hero is revealed to have harmed someone; evidence and reputation update separately.
- A player negotiates a nonviolent resolution that still requires accountability.
- An antagonist changes sides without erasing earlier consequences.
- Two factions tell incompatible stories about the same event; the source record remains inspectable.

## 9. Implementation boundary

This is a sourcebook and narrative design contract, not a villain generator, combat system, criminal planner or autonomous antagonist engine. City supplies persistent characters, institutions, relationships, locations and events; StoryBuilder and SURPS own their respective narrative and play procedures.
