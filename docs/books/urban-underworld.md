# The Urban Underworld — crews, fences, illicit markets and hidden power

**Owner:** Singularity City · **LOD:** 2 civic/economic specialization · **Status:** authored domain sourcebook; not a live criminal network simulation.

Every city has more than its official map. There are informal economies, forbidden trades, secret loyalties, protection arrangements, corrupt offices, rumor networks, gray markets and places where the powerful meet beyond public view. The underworld is not one guild with one leader. It is a shifting ecology of people, institutions, dependencies, opportunities and harm.

This book describes that ecology for fictional cities and role-playing experiences. It connects to the City [Thieves & Shadow Work](thieves-and-shadow-work.md) and the general [Nonplayer Characters sourcebook](nonplayer-characters.md). It is a systems and storytelling model, not an operational guide to organizing real criminal activity.

## 1. There is no single underworld

A city may contain overlapping networks:
- independent thieves and small crews;
- fences, brokers and buyers of disputed goods;
- smugglers and prohibited-goods traders;
- gambling rooms and informal credit;
- counterfeiters and document forgers as fictional plot roles;
- protection rackets and extortion networks;
- corrupt officials and compromised institutions;
- black-market healers, salvage brokers or forbidden scholars;
- mutual-aid networks operating outside formal institutions;
- dissidents, rebels, smugglers of people out of danger, and political undergrounds.

These groups are not morally equivalent. A mutual-aid network that violates an unjust law is not the same as a predatory extortion crew. A smuggler helping refugees escape persecution has a different purpose and consequence profile from a trafficker exploiting them. The setting's law, power structure, consent and harm matter.

## 2. Network anatomy

Represent an organization as a collection of people, roles, relationships, resources, places and events—not as a single villain record.

| Element | Description |
|---|---|
| Crew / network | A named or unnamed association with a scope and history |
| Membership | A person's current or former relationship to the group |
| Role | Broad responsibilities such as broker, collector, lookout, courier, negotiator or leader |
| Cell | A partially connected subgroup; do not assume every member knows every other member |
| Contact | A relationship for communication or exchange, not proof of membership |
| Patron | A person or institution providing money, protection or influence |
| Debt / obligation | A claimed or agreed obligation with disputed status and history |
| Territory / influence | Places where a group claims presence, access or social influence |
| Resource | Money, goods, information, favors, shelter or political leverage |
| Event | Recruitment, dispute, deal, betrayal, arrest, loss, succession or dissolution |

Membership can be voluntary, inherited, temporary, coerced, disputed or fabricated. It can end. A former member may still face obligations or danger, but that should be represented as a relationship/event, not a permanent identity flag.

## 3. Organizational patterns

Use patterns to help authors create variety:
- **Loose crew:** a small circle that forms around a specific opportunity or relationship.
- **Trade network:** brokers and specialists connected by recurring exchanges.
- **Guild-like organization:** formal ranks, dues, customs, arbitration and mutual support; may be legitimate, illicit or mixed.
- **Territorial faction:** influence tied to neighborhoods, routes, venues or community relationships.
- **Patronage network:** activity sustained by wealthy sponsors or compromised officials.
- **Cellular movement:** partially isolated groups with limited shared knowledge.
- **Family enterprise:** kinship and inheritance shape trust and succession.
- **Mutual-aid underground:** hidden coordination to provide shelter, food, information or escape from persecution.
- **Predatory racket:** control through threats, debt, coercion or violence.

These are descriptive patterns, not a blueprint for real criminal organization. Keep fictional logistics abstract; emphasize character motivations, tensions, social costs and consequences.

## 4. Places and the city's shadow geography

The underworld uses the same city as everyone else. It may be visible in:
- market stalls, pawnshops, taverns, lodging houses and workshops;
- warehouses, docks, transit hubs and freight districts;
- neglected buildings, abandoned infrastructure and contested parcels;
- private clubs, salons, counting houses and elite institutions;
- alleys, courtyards, rooftops, basements and service spaces;
- religious, cultural or community venues that may offer sanctuary;
- digital or virtual venues in advanced settings.

A location's association with an organization is time-bound and evidence-scoped. A tavern is not criminal merely because a crew meets there. A neighborhood is not inherently dangerous because crime has been recorded there. Do not label a whole district by the conduct of a few people.

City owns place identity, access policy, parcel/tenure relationships and public-space context. A specialist experience such as FSM_Dungeon owns its own interior topology when a location hands off into a dungeon or underground environment.

## 5. Fences and disputed property

A fence is a broker who buys, sells or redirects property whose provenance or ownership may be compromised. Model the object, not just its price.

For each item, preserve:
- stable item identity and description;
- origin and prior ownership claims;
- custody history and known transfers;
- provenance evidence and gaps;
- current holder and sale offer;
- whether the item is missing, stolen, recovered, seized, surrendered or disputed;
- any restitution or return event.

A buyer may acquire custody without acquiring legitimate title. A transaction does not erase a victim's claim. A recovered item may be returned, retained as evidence, or subject to a dispute. A fence may be a ruthless broker, a reluctant intermediary, an unwitting buyer or a character trying to return objects to their owners.

## 6. Black markets and the wider Economy

A black market is an exchange context whose goods, participants, terms or venue are prohibited, concealed, restricted or outside recognized oversight in a particular setting. It is not a separate physics or money system.

City supplies:
- market and venue identity;
- seller, buyer and intermediary character references;
- goods and provenance claims;
- local availability, access, time and scenario context;
- civic restrictions, disputes and incident links.

The shared [Economy package](../../ECONOMY_IN_SINGULARITY_CITY.md) owns:
- currency and account semantics;
- value transfer and settlement;
- ledger journal, receipts and deterministic replay;
- pricing primitives and supported market operations.

A black-market scenario can apply different authored prices, scarcity, risk premiums, trust and access conditions. Those are scenario inputs, not a second ledger. The same transaction evidence must remain auditable by the authoritative host even when characters conceal information from one another. Do not model secrecy by deleting the counterparty or erasing the journal.

### Economic effects

Illicit markets can:
- divert goods from ordinary supply;
- create demand for stolen or restricted items;
- raise prices through scarcity or risk;
- create debt and dependency;
- undermine legitimate merchants;
- finance corruption or coercion;
- provide otherwise unavailable goods under oppressive regimes;
- redistribute resources in ways that may help some residents and harm others.

The effect depends on the setting and the transaction. Do not assume every informal exchange is harmful or every official market is fair.

## 7. Power, protection and corruption

Power can be formal, economic, social, coercive or informational. A crew may have little money but substantial neighborhood trust. A merchant may control credit. An official may influence permits. A gang may intimidate residents but lack legitimacy. A community may refuse to cooperate with both criminals and authorities.

Represent influence as relationships and claims with provenance:
- who claims authority or protection;
- who accepts, rejects or disputes it;
- what resources or obligations support it;
- what happens when the relationship changes;
- which residents benefit, pay, resist or are harmed.

Protection can be genuine service, extortion, patronage, political cover or a mixture. The same group can provide safety to some residents while exploiting others.

## 8. Rumor, intelligence and uncertainty

Rumors are information events, not world facts. Each rumor should have an origin, time, source reliability, audience, transmission path and current status. Characters may believe false reports or act on incomplete information.

Keep separate:
- objective event record;
- witness observation;
- character belief;
- rumor as repeated;
- institutional finding;
- author/GM secret;
- player knowledge.

A hidden network should not become omniscient. A group knows what its members and contacts have learned, not everything that exists in the setting.

## 9. Conflict and change

Underworld networks change through:
- succession, arrest, disappearance, death or retirement;
- recruitment, defection and betrayal;
- internal dispute and factional split;
- changing markets, laws, technologies and routes;
- community resistance or cooperation;
- loss of patrons, venues, resources or legitimacy;
- restitution, reform, reconciliation or dissolution.

Track consequences for ordinary residents: lost wages, disrupted trade, fear, injury, displacement, price changes, damaged trust, or new community support. Conflict should change the city in persistent, inspectable ways rather than resetting when the player leaves.

## 10. Encounters and campaign hooks

- A neighborhood asks a crew to stop preying on residents, but the crew provides a service the city has neglected.
- A fence offers to return an heirloom if the player can establish its provenance.
- A guild's new leader wants to abandon violence but cannot settle old obligations.
- A corrupt official frames a rival network to hide their own dealings.
- A black-market medicine seller is the only source in a district; the campaign must confront scarcity and exploitation rather than assume simple good/evil roles.
- A mutual-aid network is mistaken for a criminal cell by an authoritarian government.
- A merchant's legal business and illicit patronage share people and places but must remain separate in the evidence record.
- A crew dissolves, leaving debts, dependents and disputed property behind.

## 12. Implementation boundary and validation

This book is a sourcebook and domain design, not a live criminal-network generator, market, investigation engine or social simulation.

Validate with:
1. a small independent crew with asymmetric knowledge;
2. a fence handling an object with disputed ownership;
3. a legitimate market and an illicit exchange sharing the same city economy;
4. a mutual-aid underground under an oppressive regime;
5. a predatory protection racket whose residents have differing relationships to it;
6. a network that dissolves while its debts, victims, property claims and events persist.

The same stable people, places, objects and Economy journal must be referenced across scenes. A new story must not create duplicate identities or erase history.
