# Justice, Courts & Restitution

*truth, process and repair*

**Sourcebook ID:** `singularity-city.domain.justice-courts-and-restitution`  
**Edition:** `0.1.0`  

**Owner:** Singularity City · **LOD:** 2 institutional specialization · **Status:** authored domain sourcebook; not a legal-advice service or adjudication engine.

Justice is not merely punishment after a rule is broken. It is the system by which a society determines what happened, whose rights and obligations are involved, which rules apply, what remedies are justified, and how decisions can be challenged. Different cultures and eras answer these questions differently. A credible city makes those differences explicit.

This book is setting-neutral. It can describe a village council, customary forum, royal court, jury system, constitutional judiciary, religious tribunal, arbitration house, restorative circle or authoritarian show trial. Describing an institution does not endorse its fairness. The model must distinguish legitimacy, procedure, outcome and the experience of the people subject to it.

## 1. Justice is a process, not a verdict field

A case may move through:
1. report, petition or referral;
2. jurisdiction and admissibility review;
3. investigation and evidence gathering;
4. notice and response by affected parties;
5. hearing, mediation or trial;
6. finding and reasons;
7. remedy, sentence or agreement;
8. appeal, review or reconsideration;
9. completion, non-completion or later reopening.

Not every dispute requires every stage. Emergency protective measures may precede a hearing, but they should have a defined basis, duration and review. A settlement may resolve a civil dispute without deciding criminal responsibility. A political pardon may change a sentence without changing the historical finding.

## 2. Keep legal categories distinct

| Category | Question |
|---|---|
| Rule / law | What standard applies, from which source and during what period? |
| Allegation | What does a party claim happened? |
| Evidence | What supports or contradicts the claim? |
| Finding | What did an authorized decision-maker conclude, under which standard? |
| Liability | Who is legally responsible for a defined harm or obligation? |
| Remedy | What should be done to address the claim or harm? |
| Sentence / sanction | What consequence is imposed under the applicable rules? |
| Appeal | What decision is being challenged, on what grounds? |
| Historical record | What was alleged, decided, changed or later overturned? |

A finding is not omniscient truth. Record the decision-maker, jurisdiction, applicable rules, standard of proof, reasons, evidence considered, dissents, date and appeal status. An overturned conviction remains part of history, but it must not continue to be presented as a current valid finding.

## 3. Courts and other forums

A city may use several forums with different jurisdiction:
- local elders or customary councils;
- civil courts for private disputes;
- criminal courts for public offenses;
- administrative tribunals for permits, benefits and regulation;
- labor and trade arbitration;
- family and child-protection forums;
- religious or cultural courts where recognized by the setting;
- constitutional or supreme courts;
- restorative processes and community mediation.

Each forum needs a defined mandate, membership or appointment process, independence, procedural rules, jurisdiction, records, enforcement relationship and review path. Avoid assuming that one modern court structure fits every civilization.

## 4. Due process and participation

The relevant safeguards depend on the setting's law and traditions, but authors should ask:
- Was the person given meaningful notice?
- Could each party understand and answer the claim?
- Was the decision-maker competent and sufficiently independent?
- Were conflicts of interest disclosed?
- Could parties present relevant evidence and challenge adverse evidence?
- Was interpretation, counsel or an advocate available where required?
- Were coercion, intimidation and unequal access considered?
- Were reasons recorded?
- Was there a meaningful route to review?

A process can be formally equal yet practically inaccessible. Wealth, status, literacy, language, disability, distance, political influence and fear can shape participation. Model those barriers rather than declaring justice fair because the same written rule applies to everyone.

## 5. Evidence and standards of proof

Evidence has provenance and limits. A record may be direct, circumstantial, testimonial, physical, documentary, digital, magical or expert-derived depending on the setting. Preserve source, collector, time, custody, transformations, uncertainty, challenge and admissibility decisions.

Standards of proof are jurisdiction-specific. Do not collapse “suspected,” “more likely than not,” “clear and convincing,” “beyond reasonable doubt,” or a fictional equivalent into one confidence score. A court's burden of proof is a rule of decision, not a statistical probability emitted by an AI.

Witnesses may be mistaken or pressured. Expert testimony may be disputed. Magical divination, predictive systems or machine-generated analysis should have explicit limits and challenge procedures; no mysterious tool should silently become an infallible truth oracle.

## 6. Remedies, sentencing and restitution

Possible outcomes include:
- declaration of rights or status;
- return of property;
- repair or replacement;
- compensation or restitution;
- apology or acknowledgment;
- mediated agreement;
- treatment, education or rehabilitation;
- restriction or supervision;
- fine or other lawful sanction;
- detention or other loss of liberty;
- dismissal, acquittal or no further action;
- institutional correction or policy change.

A remedy should connect to the finding, harm, applicable law and capacity to comply. Do not treat inability to pay as proof of bad character. Do not treat a victim's needs as satisfied merely because a sentence was imposed. Restitution can be partial, disputed, delayed or impossible; preserve that status honestly.

The shared Economy package owns accounts, currency, transfers, settlement and financial journals. A court order may authorize a payment or change in obligation, but City must not create a shadow ledger. Non-financial remedies remain first-class.

## 7. Sentencing and social consequences

Sentencing philosophies can include deterrence, incapacitation, rehabilitation, retribution, restoration, reconciliation or political control. These purposes can conflict. State which philosophy a fictional institution claims, which it actually practices, and whose interests it serves.

Track the wider consequences of a case: family separation, employment, housing, reputation, safety, public confidence, future opportunity, community repair and institutional precedent. A sentence may affect people who were not parties to the case. Those effects should not be used to erase the rights of the person harmed or the accused.

## 8. Appeals, clemency and correction

An appeal is a review under defined authority, not simply a second opinion. Record grounds, scope, reviewing body, deadlines, new evidence rules, outcome and effect on the original decision.

Clemency, pardon, amnesty, expungement, record sealing and exoneration are distinct legal acts. Their effects depend on the setting's law. Do not silently rewrite the original case history. Preserve what happened, what the institution decided, and what later changed.

## 9. Restorative and customary justice

Restorative processes may center the people harmed, those responsible, families and community. Participation must not be assumed voluntary because a meeting was arranged. Safety, coercion, power imbalance and the right to decline matter. A restorative agreement should not erase serious harm or substitute for a required public process without a lawful basis.

Customary justice can be legitimate, contested, adaptive or oppressive. Record who recognizes the forum, whose customs are represented, who is excluded, and whether an appeal or external review exists. Avoid portraying a culture as having one timeless and unanimous legal tradition.

## 10. Encounters and campaign situations

- A recovered heirloom has clear provenance, but its current possessor bought it in good faith.
- A magistrate's decision is lawful under an old charter but conflicts with a new constitution.
- A witness changes their account after receiving threats.
- A court must decide whether a magical testimony method is admissible and challengeable.
- A restorative agreement offers repair, but one party feels pressured to accept it.
- A conviction is overturned years later, after the accused has rebuilt a life elsewhere.
- A noble claims immunity while residents challenge the legal basis of the privilege.
- A court order directs compensation, but the responsible institution has no available budget.

## 12. Implementation boundary and validation

This book is an authored domain sourcebook, not a court engine, legal database, evidence platform, sentencing recommender or legal-advice product. It does not determine real legal rights.

Validate with:
1. a property dispute resolved by mediation;
2. a criminal case with disputed evidence and a recorded appeal;
3. a court with overlapping jurisdiction and a conflict-of-interest challenge;
4. a restitution order that is only partly completed;
5. a customary forum with contested representation;
6. an overturned decision whose history remains intact;
7. a case where political pressure attempts to alter an independent finding.
