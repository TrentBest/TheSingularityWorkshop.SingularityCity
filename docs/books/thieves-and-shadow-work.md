# Thieves & Shadow Work — characters who live between the rules

**Owner:** Singularity City · **LOD:** 2 specialization of Nonplayer Characters · **Status:** authored domain sourcebook; not a criminal-behavior simulator.

A thief is a character, not a prop with a pickpocket animation. They have a history, relationships, needs, talents, fears, obligations, limits, routines and choices. Some steal to survive; others for status, ideology, thrill, revenge or profit. Some are skilled professionals; others are desperate opportunists. Some belong to crews or guilds; others work alone. A convincing city makes room for all of them without reducing every poor resident to a criminal or every criminal to a villain.

This book specializes the City-owned [Nonplayer Characters sourcebook (LOD 1)](nonplayer-characters.md). It adds criminal-role context while preserving the same persistent character identity, agency, relationships and provenance. It is intended for fictional worldbuilding, tabletop campaigns, narrative design and simulation of civic consequences—not as real-world instruction for committing crimes or evading authorities.

## 1. The role is contextual, not an identity class

A character may be described as a thief because of an occupation, reputation, affiliation, past event, current allegation or player-facing role. Those are different facts.

| Facet | Example | What it does not prove |
|---|---|---|
| Self-description | Calls themself a burglar, cutpurse or scavenger | That the claim is true |
| Occupation | Makes a living through theft or illicit acquisition | That every act is theft |
| Allegation | A witness or authority accuses them | That the allegation is correct |
| Conviction / adjudication | A recognized institution records an outcome | That the record is infallible |
| Reputation | Neighbors believe the character is dangerous or dishonest | That the belief is accurate |
| Affiliation | Member of a crew, guild or network | That the member knows every activity |
| Historical event | A specific theft is attributed to the character | That it defines their whole life |

Keep identity, conduct, allegation, evidence, adjudication and reputation separate. A false accusation should not silently rewrite a character's history. A reformed thief does not lose their identity; a respected merchant may have a hidden past; a person can be falsely branded a criminal by a corrupt institution.

## 2. Archetypes and motivations

Archetypes are prompts for differentiated characters, not rigid classes or moral alignments.

- **The opportunist:** acts when temptation, need or circumstance outweighs perceived risk.
- **The professional:** treats illicit work as a trade, with standards, boundaries and a reputation.
- **The confidence artist:** relies on social access, trust, performance and manipulation as narrative themes; portray consequences and victims, not a how-to.
- **The burglar:** associated with unlawful entry and property loss; represent the event and its aftermath without procedural entry instructions.
- **The cutpurse / pickpocket:** a street-level thief whose victims may be travelers, market-goers or the wealthy; keep mechanics abstract and non-instructional.
- **The fence:** broker who connects stolen property to buyers, creating a relationship between theft, demand and the wider economy.
- **The smuggler:** moves prohibited or restricted goods across a boundary; the setting defines what is prohibited and why.
- **The scavenger:** recovers abandoned, discarded or contested resources; distinguish salvage rights from theft.
- **The highway robber / raider:** uses threat or force against travelers; this creates fear, route disruption and public costs.
- **The inside associate:** abuses entrusted access or knowledge; trust, authority and institutional accountability matter.
- **The reluctant participant:** coerced by debt, threat, dependency or protection obligations.
- **The reforming character:** attempts to leave an old network, repair harm and build a different livelihood.

A character may occupy multiple roles over time. The book should support contradictions: a generous neighbor who steals, a violent crew member who protects one person, or a principled outlaw whose code still harms innocent people.

## 3. Character facets for shadow work

Extend the LOD 1 character profile only where the story needs it.

### Motive and pressure
Record immediate need, long-term ambition, coercion, debt, loyalty, fear, ideology, status, addiction or dependency where relevant to the authored story. Do not infer criminality from poverty, homelessness, culture, appearance or neighborhood.

### Capabilities and limits
Represent broad fictional competencies such as disguise, social persuasion, street knowledge, appraisal, forgery as a plot capability, concealment as an abstract narrative capability, negotiation, intimidation, escape under pressure or knowledge of a local network. Record training, access, equipment availability, confidence and limitations separately. This vocabulary is for character differentiation and adjudication; it must not become a real-world procedural guide.

### Knowledge and secrets
A character may know a contact, rumor, market reputation, safe haven, debt, allegation or hidden relationship. Knowledge is scoped to the character and has a source, time, confidence and possible error. The world model must not leak secrets to players merely because they exist in the database.

### Relationships
Track victims, witnesses, accomplices, rivals, employers, fences, informants, protectors, family, dependents, reformers and authorities. Relationships can be asymmetric, contested, coerced or concealed. A contact is not automatically an accomplice.

### Boundaries and code
Some characters refuse violence, exploitation of children, betrayal, trafficking, harm to a community or jobs that violate a personal code. Others have no such boundaries. A code is a motive and constraint, not proof of virtue or safety.

## 4. A life beyond the crime

Thieves sleep, eat, work, love, argue, raise families, attend festivals, pay rent, get sick, repair relationships and have ordinary days. Their lives connect to the same City systems as everyone else's:
- home, shelter, lodging and neighborhood;
- legal and informal work, skills and services;
- family, friendship, dependents and community ties;
- shops, markets, taverns, transport and public spaces;
- debt, savings, wages, purchases and property;
- health, injury, safety, justice and civic services.

Do not make the entire underworld operate continuously for the player's benefit. Most members have routines and competing obligations. A crew may be inactive, divided, celebrating, grieving, negotiating or simply trying to get through the week.

## 5. Crime as an event with consequences

Represent an incident as a time-bounded event with:
- stable event identity and time/place references;
- alleged or established conduct, expressed at an appropriate level;
- affected people, property, institutions and services;
- actor references with attribution status (unknown, alleged, witnessed, admitted, adjudicated);
- evidence references and their custody/provenance;
- loss, damage, injury or disruption, including unknown values;
- response, investigation, dispute and resolution events;
- restitution, recovery, compensation or unresolved harm.

Do not equate suspicion with proof. Do not make a missing item disappear from the economic model without a corresponding event. Do not silently turn recovered goods into legitimate inventory: ownership and custody disputes remain explicit.

## 6. Player-facing design

A thief character can support many kinds of play:
- investigate a theft and compare witness accounts;
- negotiate with a fence to recover a stolen heirloom;
- protect a neighborhood from an exploitative crew;
- choose whether to accept a coercive debt;
- help a character leave a gang;
- expose corruption or discover that an accusation is false;
- play a thief balancing loyalty, survival and harm;
- rebuild trust through restitution and changed behavior.

The character's agency should remain intact. A player may persuade, help, oppose or befriend a thief; they do not own the character's decisions unless the experience's control contract explicitly says so.

## 7. Civic response and justice

City owns the relationship between incidents and civic institutions: reporting, patrol coverage, public safety services, licensing, courts or local equivalents, detention places where applicable, community mediation, restitution and rehabilitation programs. The relevant institutions vary by reality, era and setting.

The police or guard service is not omniscient. Institutions can be under-resourced, biased, corrupt, accountable, legitimate, contested or absent. Different witnesses may disagree. A city can have strong public order and still be unjust; an informal community can provide protection while also exerting coercive control.

Detailed investigation procedure, law, evidence standards, sentencing and restorative justice belong in a dedicated civic justice sourcebook and applicable SURPS setting rulebooks.

## 8. Economy integration

The shared [Economy package](../../ECONOMY_IN_SINGULARITY_CITY.md) remains the authority for currency, accounts, ledger operations, receipts and value movement. City supplies context:
- who is buying, selling, employed, owed or affected;
- where an exchange is offered or a transaction takes place;
- which goods are claimed, missing, recovered, seized, damaged or disputed;
- which local institutions, laws and scenario rules apply.

A stolen object is not a new object. Its provenance and ownership claim persist. A fence's purchase may transfer custody without resolving title. A recovered item may return to its owner, be held as evidence or remain disputed. A black-market price is an authored market condition, not proof that the sale is lawful or that the seller owns the item.

Do not create a separate criminal ledger or an untraceable currency to represent illicit activity. Use the same Economy semantics and preserve event links, counterparty identities where known, receipts and audit evidence. Fictional secrecy can affect character knowledge and presentation; it must not erase authoritative provenance.

## 9. Safety, dignity and narrative boundaries

- Do not use real-world criminal stereotypes as shortcuts for ethnicity, class, disability, housing status or culture.
- Do not make victimization a decorative city flavor. Record the affected person and consequences.
- Do not provide operational real-world instructions for theft, intrusion, fraud, evasion, fencing or violence.
- Keep fictional capabilities abstract and scenario-bound.
- Distinguish consensual roleplay, fictional crime, allegations and real-world misconduct.
- Do not turn a character's criminal role into permission for abuse by players or other characters.
- Trusted hosts own authentication, authorization and enforcement; a fictional alias, guild rank or character skill is never a digital permission.

## 11. Validation scenarios

1. A hungry apprentice steals food, is identified incorrectly, and the actual offender is later discovered.
2. A professional thief returns a stolen heirloom after learning its significance.
3. A fence buys an object whose title remains disputed; Economy records value movement while City preserves custody and ownership claims.
4. A crew pressures a member through debt; the character seeks a way out.
5. A wealthy merchant commits theft through abuse of entrusted access; status does not shield the character from evidence.
6. A neighborhood protects a vulnerable resident while disagreeing about whether to cooperate with authorities.
7. A player-controlled thief changes to host control without losing identity, relationships or unresolved consequences.

## 12. Implementation boundary

This is an authored domain sourcebook, not an implemented thief AI, crime generator, investigation engine, black-market simulator or justice system. The concepts should first be represented as provenance-bearing City records and tested scenarios. Any future runtime must use shared Ontology and Economy contracts rather than inventing local parallel identities or ledgers.
