# Göbekli Tepe — Research Pilot

![The Singularity Workshop animated moniker](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TrentBest/TheSingularityWorkshop.Visualize/development/docs/assets/moniker.svg)

**Status:** Research pilot — not a completed sourcebook

## Why this is the first deep-time pilot

Göbekli Tepe is an excellent test of the City's evidence-first, scan-first approach because it is a real archaeological site with monumental remains, an active research programme, and multiple competing questions about chronology, architecture, landscape, and use.

**This is an archaeological-site sourcebook, not a claim that Göbekli Tepe was a city.** The available evidence supports describing a complex prehistoric site with monumental enclosures and domestic structures; urban status, a single purpose, and a simple social model must not be assumed.

The place belongs to the real-world reality set. A digital capture of the site today and an evidence-based reconstruction of its earlier phases are different representations of the same real place. An intentionally altered alternate-history version would be fiction. See [Reality Sets, Place Identity and Historical Reconstruction](../../../REALITY_AND_RECONSTRUCTION.md).

## Scope and current baseline

- **Place:** Göbekli Tepe, Şanlıurfa Province, Türkiye.
- **Subject type:** archaeological site and cultural landscape; not pre-classified as a city.
- **Research focus:** extant site, monumental architecture, T-shaped pillars, domestic structures, landscape setting, construction histories, chronology, and the evidence for activity beyond the excavated enclosures.
- **Public baseline:** UNESCO describes monumental round-oval and rectangular megalithic structures associated with the Pre-Pottery Neolithic, broadly dated 9,600–8,200 BCE. UNESCO also notes domestic structures identified through more recent excavation.
- **Protection context:** UNESCO lists a 126 ha property and a 461 ha buffer zone; inscription took place in 2018.
- **Important limitation:** a broad period range is not a precise construction date for an individual building or pillar. Recent stratigraphic and building-archaeology work refines earlier models; each phase must be dated from the relevant evidence and publication.

Primary starting points:

## The modern site: a scan-first visit

The first experience should let a visitor explore the site as it exists today, before asking them to imagine the past.

Public research descriptions confirm that digital survey methods are being used at Göbekli Tepe:

- The Austrian Archaeological Institute's geoarchaeological project describes a multi-method investigation of the site and wider landscape, including geophysics, UAV LiDAR, aerial photography, photogrammetry, GIS and 3D modelling.
- Its published method description identifies a UAV LiDAR system using a RIEGL miniVUX-3UAV scanner and APX-20 GNSS/IMU positioning equipment to record topography and support digital terrain models.
- The same project reports a first field season in September–October 2025 and preliminary indications of additional architectural structures outside previously excavated areas.
- The German Archaeological Institute's 2026 scholarly volume includes a chapter specifically titled **“Scanning and 3D-modelling of the T-pillars at Göbekli Tepe.”** The publication record says the volume covers material uncovered between 1995 and 2023. Its PDF is listed as available in July 2027; the print edition is listed separately.

These sources establish that relevant survey and 3D-documentation work exists. They do **not** establish that raw point clouds, full-site laser scans, pillar meshes, georeferenced terrain data or reusable digital twins are publicly downloadable under a license that permits redistribution. No such dataset or license has yet been verified for this repository.

### Data access request

The next research step is to ask the responsible research teams and heritage authorities whether educational access or collaboration is possible. Request, where available:

1. georeferenced UAV LiDAR point clouds and derived terrain models;
2. pillar scans, photogrammetry, textured meshes and the corresponding raw or minimally processed captures;
3. excavation-area plans, orthophotos, survey control points, GIS layers and documented site boundaries;
4. metadata for coordinate reference systems, horizontal/vertical datums, units, registration, point spacing, accuracy, coverage and capture dates;
5. publication and redistribution terms for original data and derivative display assets;
6. restrictions on sensitive areas, unpublished excavation data, visitor safety, conservation and cultural heritage.

Do not infer that a public photograph or a rendered 3D figure grants access to the underlying scan. Do not scrape, reproduce or redistribute copyrighted scans, plans, photographs or book figures without permission. If raw data cannot be redistributed, the project may still be able to link to authoritative viewers, use licensed derivatives, or create an independently documented educational representation.

## Historical reconstruction: evidence before scenery

A past-state model must be built from archaeological records and attributed scholarship, not by making a modern scan look ancient.

The first reconstruction should be limited to a declared archaeological phase and should preserve the difference between:

- excavated and measured remains;
- structures or features identified through geophysics or survey but not excavated;
- published architectural and stratigraphic interpretations;
- City/editorial synthesis;
- authored visual reconstruction;
- unknown or disputed elements.

The 2026 DAI volume is an important new source for the T-pillar imagery and the buildings A–D, F and H. Its chapter list explicitly separates the site and stratigraphy, methods and limits of image interpretation, individual building contexts, competing interpretive approaches, and scanning/3D modelling. That structure is a useful model for how this sourcebook should treat evidence and interpretation as related but distinct.

A 2020 study by Gil Haklay and Avi Gopher, [“Geometry and Architectural Planning at Göbekli Tepe, Turkey”](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0959774319000660), presents a preliminary formal analysis of architectural planning and design processes. It is a scholarly interpretation to compare with excavation and building-archaeology evidence, not a definitive reconstruction recipe.

UNESCO's description refers to monumental communal buildings/enclosures and says their use was probably associated with social events and rituals, possibly funerary in nature. The same listing notes domestic structures. The sourcebook must not reduce these findings to “a temple” or assert one settled purpose for the whole site.

## Construction and founding as an explorable process

The long-term experience should let a learner investigate how the place was selected, built, used and changed. The first version should not invent a complete construction story.

Potential learning sequences, each requiring its own evidence ledger, include:

- the limestone plateau and the relationship between geology, topography and material availability;
- quarrying and shaping limestone pillars, where archaeological and experimental evidence support the proposed methods;
- moving and raising large stone elements, with alternative methods and explicit assumptions;
- constructing walls, benches and enclosure forms as documented for individual buildings;
- the sequence of building, modification, use, infilling and later activity;
- the relationship between monumental structures, domestic buildings and wider activity zones;
- the changing archaeological landscape and the history of excavation, conservation and visitor access.

A playable construction sequence is a hypothesis-testing lesson, not evidence that a specific prehistoric crew used that exact method. Alternative plausible sequences should remain available when the record does not decide between them.

## Planned experience layers

1. **Orientation:** location, modern access, landscape context, site boundaries and what the visitor is looking at.
2. **Present-day digital visit:** scan/survey-backed surfaces and structures, with capture coverage and data limitations visible.
3. **Archaeological evidence mode:** excavated plans, pillar records, stratigraphy, geophysical indications and citations.
4. **Time-slice reconstruction:** a selected period with element-level confidence and alternative interpretations.
5. **Construction investigation:** interactive, explicitly hypothetical methods for quarrying, moving, shaping and raising stone.
6. **Landscape investigation:** the relationship between the site, topography, resources, waterways, ecology and nearby activity zones.
7. **Educator mode:** objectives, vocabulary, source comparisons, teacher notes and learner investigations.

The same stable place and feature identities should connect the sourcebook, source ledger, scan, map, reconstruction and interactive scene. The shared reference ontology owns reality membership and semantic identity; SURPS supplies roleplaying-world composition; City owns place and civic/spatial content; Physics owns physical terrain and physical simulation; Renderer and Visualize present the evidence and experience.

## Open questions

- Which current scan, LiDAR and photogrammetry datasets can the research teams share or expose through authoritative viewers?
- What coordinate systems, datums, registration accuracy and coverage apply to each dataset?
- Which architectural phases and individual buildings have the strongest current evidence for a first reconstruction?
- Which construction hypotheses are supported by experimental archaeology and current building-archaeology studies?
- Which features or areas should not be exposed at high spatial precision for conservation or cultural-heritage reasons?
- Which specialists are willing to review the chronology, architectural reconstruction, imagery interpretation and educational presentation?
