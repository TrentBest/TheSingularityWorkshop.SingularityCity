# Governance & Civic Administration — how a city makes and delivers decisions

**Owner:** Singularity City · **LOD:** 2 institutional specialization · **Status:** authored domain sourcebook; not a live municipal operating system.

Governance is the enduring arrangement through which a community makes collective decisions, allocates responsibilities, maintains shared systems and holds officeholders answerable. Government is one part of governance; families, guilds, customary authorities, cooperatives, religious bodies, neighborhood associations and informal networks can also shape collective life.

This book treats governance as a set of institutions, mandates, decision rights, resources, procedures and relationships. It is designed to scale from a village assembly to a metropolitan federation, imperial administration, orbital habitat or planetary authority.

## 1. Governance is not one office

A city may contain:
- an elected council or assembly;
- an executive, mayor, governor, steward or ruling council;
- administrative departments and civil servants;
- courts and independent tribunals;
- public utilities and service operators;
- districts, wards and neighborhood bodies;
- customary, Indigenous, religious or treaty-based authorities;
- public corporations, cooperatives and contracted providers;
- auditors, ombuds offices and independent commissions.

These bodies may cooperate, compete, overlap or refuse recognition of one another. A title does not itself establish authority. Every office needs a source, scope, term, succession rule and accountability path.

## 2. The institutional charter

Represent an institution with:
- stable identity, name and institutional type;
- founding authority and governing instrument;
- purpose, mandate and public obligations;
- jurisdiction and limits;
- decision rights and required approvals;
- membership, appointments and term rules;
- leadership, positions and qualifications;
- resources, facilities and budget relationships;
- records, transparency and reporting duties;
- oversight, complaint and review mechanisms;
- continuity, dissolution and succession procedures.

An institution can persist while its officeholders change. An officeholder can resign without dissolving the office. A department can be reorganized while its historical records and outstanding obligations remain traceable.

## 3. Levels of authority and subsidiarity

Governance can be organized at household, neighborhood, village, city, regional, national, imperial, planetary or other scales. Responsibilities may be centralized, delegated, shared or reserved.

For each responsibility ask:
- Which level is closest to the people affected?
- Does it have the capacity and resources to act?
- Are there cross-boundary effects?
- Which rights or standards must remain protected?
- Who resolves a conflict between levels?
- Can a local body appeal or refuse an unlawful direction?

Do not assume centralization is always efficient or local control is always fair. Scale, capacity, capture, coordination and accountability all matter.

## 4. Public administration and service delivery

Administrative functions may include:
- civil registration and public records;
- land and building administration;
- water, sanitation, energy and waste;
- roads, transit and public space;
- housing, health, education and social services;
- emergency management and public safety;
- licensing, inspection and regulation;
- procurement, maintenance and capital works;
- libraries, archives, culture and recreation.

For each service, model the responsible institution, service standard, eligibility, coverage, capacity, hours, dependencies, funding, performance evidence and complaint route. A service can be formally available but inaccessible because of distance, cost, language, disability, documentation or discrimination.

City owns civic service relationships and place context. Specialist domains own the technical operation of water networks, energy systems, medical care, education or transport where those contracts exist. City should connect the systems, not duplicate their engines.

## 5. Public finance and the budget cycle

A public budget is a political and administrative decision about shared resources. It connects:
- revenue sources and legal authority to collect them;
- appropriations and spending limits;
- departments, programs and service commitments;
- procurement, contracts and capital projects;
- debt, grants, reserves and contingent liabilities;
- reporting periods, audit and public explanation.

The Economy package owns currency, accounts, transactions and financial journals. City owns the civic purpose, responsible institution, approved budget context, beneficiaries and service outcomes. A budget authorization is not itself a completed payment; a payment is not proof that a service was delivered.

Make opportunity cost visible. Funding one project can delay another. A balanced ledger does not prove that a budget is equitable or effective.

## 6. Public decisions and administrative records

A decision record should identify:
- decision identity and time;
- body and participants;
- source of authority and jurisdiction;
- question considered and options;
- evidence and public submissions;
- conflicts of interest and recusals;
- vote, consensus or other decision method;
- reasons, conditions and dissent;
- implementation owner, deadline and review date;
- appeal, amendment or repeal history.

Distinguish proposal, consultation, adopted policy, binding law, administrative instruction, contract and operational action. A public comment is not a vote. A policy announcement is not an appropriation. A council resolution does not automatically change an external specialist system.

## 7. Transparency, records and privacy

Public accountability needs accessible records, but not every personal record should be public. Define publication rules, protected information, retention, correction, audit access, lawful disclosure and historical archives.

Publish institutional decisions, spending, performance and conflicts of interest where appropriate. Protect private resident information, confidential health details, sensitive witness identities and security-critical credentials. The public record should explain the institution's actions without exposing people unnecessarily.

## 8. Accountability and institutional failure

Accountability can include elections, hearings, audits, judicial review, inspectors, ombuds offices, public petitions, recall, professional discipline, independent commissions and community oversight.

Model failure modes:
- unclear or overlapping mandates;
- capture by wealth, faction or family;
- corruption and patronage;
- understaffing and skill loss;
- inaccessible services;
- discriminatory allocation;
- fragmented records;
- emergency powers that never expire;
- inability to maintain assets;
- institutional denial or deliberate opacity.

A city can have elaborate formal institutions and still lack effective governance. Compare written rules with actual practice, who benefits, who can challenge decisions and what happens when the institution fails.

## 9. Constitutional and non-constitutional orders

Governance forms include democratic, republican, monarchical, aristocratic, theocratic, oligarchic, colonial, military, technocratic, federated, confederal, communal, corporate and hybrid arrangements. These are descriptive families, not a quality ranking. A regime's self-description may differ from its actual distribution of power.

For each setting document:
- source of ultimate authority;
- how leaders are selected and removed;
- limits on office and emergency powers;
- representation and participation;
- lawmaking and adjudication relationships;
- succession and continuity;
- recognition of local and minority institutions;
- peaceful dispute and transfer mechanisms.

## 10. Encounters and campaign situations

- A village assembly must allocate a scarce water supply between households and a mill.
- A city department has a mandate but no budget or qualified staff.
- A regional authority orders a project that displaces a neighborhood.
- An audit reveals that a celebrated public work was never completed.
- A mayor's emergency order has no clear expiration or review.
- A district has formal representation but cannot access the meeting place.
- A treaty-based authority disputes the city's claim to jurisdiction.
- A department's records are lost during a change of administration.

## 12. Implementation boundary and validation

This is a domain sourcebook and authoring contract, not a functioning government simulator, public-finance engine, voting service or municipal records platform.

Validate with:
1. a village assembly with customary and elected responsibilities;
2. a city with a council, executive, departments and independent courts;
3. a federal dispute over jurisdiction;
4. a budget proposal that changes service coverage;
5. an audit that corrects a public claim without deleting history;
6. an emergency power with a clear sunset and review;
7. a department that is dissolved while its obligations and records transfer to a successor.
