# City Sourcebooks — Places, Settlements and Their Histories

![The Singularity Workshop animated moniker](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/TrentBest/TheSingularityWorkshop.Visualize/development/docs/assets/moniker.svg)

## Purpose

Reality membership is a shared-ontology concern, not a City-local classification. A real place, an evidence-based reconstruction of its past, and a fictional adaptation must remain distinguishable; the sourcebook policy is defined in [Reality Sets, Place Identity and Historical Reconstruction](REALITY_AND_RECONSTRUCTION.md).


Singularity City should publish books about both the systems that make settlements work and the particular places those systems produce.

A **domain sourcebook** explains a reusable subject: residents, commerce, transport, utilities, governance, buildings or settlement formation. A **place sourcebook** studies one identifiable place or connected landscape: a village, town, city, metropolitan region, historical settlement, archaeological site, planned community, monumental/ritual landscape, orbital habitat or fictional city.

The subject is therefore larger than cities that still exist. The catalogue should reach across time and geography: from early monumental and settled landscapes to ancient urban civilizations, medieval towns, Indigenous cities, industrial centers and future megacities. Some places will be cities; others, such as Göbekli Tepe, are important precisely because the evidence does not justify casually calling them cities.

These are complementary views, not competing taxonomies. A Tenochtitlan book can use general mobility, commerce, resident and civic models while documenting the Mexica capital's own geography, history, institutions and distinctive systems.

Place sourcebooks are intended to serve as:
- research-led introductions and deeper reference works;
- visual atlases and historical comparisons;
- educational material for geography, history, archaeology, architecture, ecology and civic systems;
- worldbuilding references for SURPS and other experiences;
- authored, provenance-bearing inputs that may later inform City fixtures and simulations;
- entry points into interactive learning experiences where students explore how a place functioned and how we know.

A book is not itself a live city model. Narrative, diagrams, maps, researched claims, authored fixtures, derived analyses and runtime state must remain distinguishable.

## Two axes: subject and place

| Axis | Question | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Domain | What kind of system or subject are we studying? | Nonplayer Characters, Villages to Cities, mobility, markets, water, governance |
| Place | Which particular settlement, site or region are we studying? | Tacoma, Tenochtitlan, Troy, Göbekli Tepe, Uruk, Angkor |

A place book may link to many domain books. A domain book may be demonstrated by many places. Neither should copy the other's authority.

## The catalogue spans different kinds of places

Do not force every subject into a modern city template. Assign a clear subject classification and explain why it fits.

- **Living cities and settlements:** places with contemporary communities, institutions and changing urban form.
- **Historical cities:** places documented through written, visual, archaeological and environmental evidence.
- **Archaeological settlements:** places studied primarily through material remains, stratigraphy, landscape and scientific analysis.
- **Monumental and ritual landscapes:** complexes whose function, population and relationship to settlement are still debated.
- **Cultural landscapes and settlement networks:** connected places, routes, agricultural systems, ceremonial centers and regional relationships.
- **Legendary, literary and contested places:** subjects where archaeology, historical records, oral traditions and literature must be kept in distinct evidence lanes.
- **Fictional and speculative places:** authored worlds, with canon, creator additions and speculation clearly distinguished.

A site can belong to more than one category, and the classification can change as research develops. Do not infer a dense permanent population, urban status, political structure or single purpose from monumental architecture alone.

## What a place sourcebook contains

A place book should build a layered account, choosing detail appropriate to the subject and evidence available.

1. **Identity and scope** — names, aliases, historical names, cultural context, jurisdiction or archaeological designation, geographic extent, neighboring places, and what the book means by the subject's name.
2. **Geographic setting** — landforms, water, climate, ecology, hazards and regional position, with physical facts sourced from the relevant authoritative domain.
3. **Chronology and historical periods** — formation, occupation, construction phases, major transformations, continuity, abandonment, reuse, rupture, contested accounts and dated snapshots.
4. **Settlement and spatial form** — centers, districts, streets, blocks, parcels, public spaces, building patterns, density, monumental structures, settlement edges and regional connections where evidence supports them.
5. **People and communities** — residents, migration, languages, communities, occupations, daily life, diet, health and demographic evidence, with historical context, ethical care and uncertainty explicit.
6. **Institutions and collective organization** — governance, authority, public works, law, ritual, civic participation, political organization and institutional interpretations; do not assume modern equivalents.
7. **Movement and connection** — walking, roads, paths, transit, rail, ports, airports, waterways, freight, pilgrimage, trade and regional networks.
8. **Provisioning and infrastructure** — water, sanitation, energy, food, waste, communications, housing, health, education, craft production, agriculture and resilience.
9. **Work, exchange and culture** — markets, industries, crafts, institutions, food, art, architecture, festivals, memory and public life.
10. **Landmarks and significant places** — why a place matters, how it relates to its surroundings, what is known about access and use, and what remains unknown.
11. **Challenges and change** — growth, decline, displacement, conflict, environmental pressure, disasters, investment, adaptation and competing futures.
12. **Source ledger and interpretation** — references, dates, coverage, confidence, conflicts, derived measures and authored scenarios.

Not every place has evidence for every topic. An absent record is not permission to invent a plausible answer.

## Archaeological and deep-time evidence

Archaeological sourcebooks require an especially explicit method because the surviving evidence is incomplete and uneven.

For each major reconstruction, distinguish:
- **Observed evidence:** excavated structures, artifacts, inscriptions, stratigraphy, human and animal remains, botanical evidence, residues, landscape features and dated samples.
- **Published interpretation:** what named researchers or institutions infer from that evidence, including the date and context of the interpretation.
- **Competing interpretations:** credible alternatives, disagreements and the evidence each interpretation relies on.
- **Book synthesis:** our own comparison or explanation, labelled as synthesis rather than a new discovery.
- **Visual reconstruction:** an illustration or model translating an interpretation into a scene; it is not itself evidence.
- **Unknowns:** areas where the record cannot currently support a confident conclusion.

Record dating methods and ranges where relevant, including what was actually dated (for example, a sample, layer, structure or associated material). Do not turn a broad archaeological period into a falsely precise construction date. Explain preservation and sampling bias: what survives is not necessarily representative of what once existed.

For ancient and prehistoric sites, use a confidence vocabulary with a short explanation and source basis rather than a naked percentage. A reconstruction may be high-confidence in its topography but low-confidence in the exact appearance, social meaning or use of a structure.

### Examples that belong in this programme

These are editorial examples and research targets, not claims that books are complete:

| Place / subject | Why it matters | Editorial caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Göbekli Tepe** | Monumental architecture and activity in a deep-prehistory context; a powerful case for exploring what archaeology can and cannot establish | Do not simply label it a city, temple, or single-purpose site as settled fact; explain current debates and the limits of evidence |
| **Tenochtitlan** | Mexica capital, island-city setting, causeways, water management, markets, political and ceremonial life | Distinguish Mexica-era evidence, Indigenous accounts and knowledge, early colonial descriptions, later scholarship and modern reconstructions |
| **Troy / Hisarlık** | Multiple settlement layers and the relationship between archaeology and the Homeric tradition | Do not collapse the excavated site's phases into one city or equate archaeological evidence with every detail of epic narrative |
| **Uruk** | Early urban development, monumental architecture, administration and writing | Explain chronology, regional context and how scholars define urbanism |
| **Çatalhöyük** | Dense Neolithic settlement and household/ritual interpretations | Avoid presenting one model of social organization or ritual meaning as certain |
| **Mohenjo-daro** | Indus urban planning, water and sanitation infrastructure, craft and exchange | The script remains undeciphered; avoid unsupported claims about rulers, language or political structure |
| **Teotihuacan** | Large planned ancient city, monumental avenue, neighborhoods and regional influence | Separate evidence for urban form from unresolved questions about political organization and language |
| **Cahokia** | Major pre-Columbian urban/ceremonial center and regional settlement network in North America | Center Indigenous histories and avoid treating the site as an isolated mystery |
| **Great Zimbabwe** | Monumental stone architecture, trade networks and African political history | Reject colonial-era distortions; ground interpretation in African archaeology and scholarship |
| **Angkor** | Urban, hydraulic, religious and landscape systems at regional scale | Treat the urban complex as a changing network, not only a temple postcard |
| **Pompeii** | Exceptional evidence for streets, homes, commerce and daily life at a particular moment | Volcanic preservation is unusual; do not treat it as a typical Roman city in every respect |

The catalogue should also include villages, Indigenous and historically marginalized places, industrial communities, planned settlements, declining cities and places outside familiar North American/European examples. Respect community authority, cultural context, privacy, descendant communities and restrictions around sensitive sites.

## Time is part of place identity

A place changes. A sourcebook must state which period a claim describes.

Useful forms include:
- **Contemporary profile:** a dated snapshot with an explicit research cutoff.
- **Historical volume:** a particular period, with boundaries and names appropriate to that time.
- **Transformation series:** comparable snapshots showing how a place changed.
- **Archaeological phase volume:** a defined occupation, construction or depositional phase, with dating uncertainty.
- **Future scenario:** a clearly labelled projection or authored possibility, never presented as observed fact.
- **Cross-era atlas:** several periods connected through stable place identities where that continuity is justified.

Historical boundaries, names, populations, street networks and institutions must not be silently projected backward or forward. A present-day administrative boundary is not automatically the right frame for an ancient settlement. Where boundaries, chronology or continuity are uncertain, preserve that uncertainty.

## Evidence and provenance standard

Every substantive claim should be classifiable as one of:

- **Sourced fact:** supported by a cited source with publication/record date and relevant period.
- **Source interpretation:** a historian's, archaeologist's, institution's or other author's interpretation, attributed as such.
- **Derived analysis:** a calculation or comparison with named inputs and method.
- **Authored reconstruction:** a reasoned but not directly evidenced reconstruction, explicitly labelled.
- **Scenario:** an intentional fictional, alternate-history or future construction.
- **Unknown / disputed:** evidence is absent, incomplete or conflicting.

Maps, plans, scans, photographs, models and images need their own provenance: creator, source, date, license or usage basis, geographic and temporal coverage, coordinate reference where relevant, transformations and whether the item is an original record, analytical map or interpretive reconstruction. Do not imply that a decorative illustration is a surveyed map or that a reconstruction is a photograph of the past.

Prefer primary and authoritative sources where suitable: excavation reports, peer-reviewed archaeology, museum and heritage records, archives, libraries, historical maps, inscriptions and translations with editorial context, scientific datasets, environmental studies, municipal and regional open-data portals, census/statistical agencies, planning documents and transport agencies. Secondary sources remain useful, especially when they explain contested history or synthesize evidence. Record disagreements rather than hiding them.

For Indigenous histories and culturally sensitive places, engage authoritative community sources and descendant-community perspectives where available. Do not publish precise locations or sensitive knowledge when access restrictions or community guidance advise against it.

## Education: books that become explorable lessons

Education is a design goal from the beginning, not a marketing layer added after the sourcebooks are written.

A sourcebook should be usable at multiple levels:
- **Orientation:** where the place is, when it existed, and what makes it distinctive.
- **Guided exploration:** inspect a street, house, market, water system, monument, farm or route.
- **Systems learning:** follow water, food, materials, people, trade, governance and movement through connected models.
- **Evidence literacy:** compare a source with an interpretation and a reconstruction; ask what evidence would change the model.
- **Investigation:** let learners test a hypothesis, compare periods or change a clearly labelled scenario.
- **Creation:** build a settlement or modify a system while seeing the consequences and assumptions.

Renderer can explain identities, relationships, evidence and reasoning in a textual form; Visualize can present maps, diagrams, scenes and spatial experiences. Both should share stable concepts and provenance. A beautiful reconstruction must never conceal uncertainty.

Design for educators and different ages without reducing the underlying subject to a single grade level. Provide teacher context, learning objectives, vocabulary, source notes, discussion prompts and activities when the material warrants them. Avoid turning culturally significant places into theme-park caricatures. Make clear whose history is being presented and whose evidence or interpretation is represented.

The long-term opportunity is that a learner can move from a book into an interactive place, inspect how it works, and become familiar with the technology by using it. The educational experience should teach both the historical subject and the general concepts—spatial reasoning, systems, evidence, infrastructure, ecology, exchange and civic life—without pretending that the model is the past itself.

## Folder and artifact convention

Place-specific books live under `docs/books/places/`, separate from reusable domain books.

Suggested structure:

```text
docs/books/places/
  README.md
  tacoma/
    README.md
    tacoma.book.json
    chapters/
    maps/
    images/
    data/
    sources/
    scenarios/
  gobekli-tepe/
    README.md
    gobekli-tepe.book.json
    chapters/
    maps/
    images/
    data/
    sources/
    scenarios/
```

A place may use a different chapter structure when its history, geography or evidence warrants it. The stable place ID and sourcebook descriptor should not depend on folder name or display title.

A descriptor should identify at least:
- schema version and stable book ID;
- category and subject type (for example, city, settlement, archaeological-site or cultural-landscape);
- title, version and status;
- place identity and geographic scope;
- temporal coverage, dating basis and research cutoff;
- cover and LOD artwork when available;
- publisher and moniker;
- source ledger location, evidence conventions and license notes.

Do not add empty placeholder chapters or pretend a sourcebook is complete merely because its directory exists. Start with a scoped, useful first edition and grow it as evidence and review become available.

## Relationship to LOD

LOD is a statement about the detail of representation, not a substitute for evidence quality, chronology or geographic scope. A regional overview may be low detail but well sourced; a single building study may be high detail but have important unknowns.

Use LOD to explain how much detail a reader or runtime should expect:
- broad regional / site orientation;
- settlement plan and landscape;
- districts, neighborhoods and routes;
- streets, blocks, public spaces and building groups;
- individual sites, buildings, rooms and artifacts where supported.

State what each level actually contains. Do not assign a higher LOD badge simply because a subject is famous or the book is longer. A high-detail reconstruction may still contain low-confidence elements, which must remain visible as such.

## Relationship to City implementation

The sourcebook is a research and authoring layer. City runtime data remains separately versioned and typed.

- **Physics** owns terrain and physical simulation; place books may cite and visualize authoritative outputs without recreating them.
- **Singularity City** owns civic geography, districts, parcels, places, routes, institutions and place relationships.
- **Economy** owns economic models, exchange, prices and valuation.
- **SURPS** owns shared ontology and composable rulebook conventions once its common contract is accepted.
- **Specialist repositories** own their domain models and operational evidence.
- **Renderer / Visualize** present the same identities at different representational levels; illustrations do not become authoritative state.

A sourcebook may propose a City fixture, but imported research, authored interpretation and simulated output must remain separately labelled. A researched historical map does not automatically become the current city blueprint. City should not invent missing archaeological facts to satisfy a runtime schema.

## First implementation sequence

1. Establish the place-book convention and keep it distinct from domain sourcebooks.
2. Establish reusable descriptor and evidence-ledger conventions when the first real book is ready to author.
3. Select a pilot place with a clear scope and accessible source base. A contemporary city and an archaeological site test different parts of the method; the programme should eventually demonstrate both.
4. Build a source-cited first edition with a clear research cutoff, chronology, geography, spatial form, daily life, systems and source ledger.
5. Add dated maps and visual comparisons with clear licenses, coordinate references and provenance.
6. For archaeological subjects, include competing interpretations and confidence explanations before creating detailed visual reconstructions.
7. Only then derive selected, explicitly labelled City fixtures from the researched material.
8. Expand to other places and periods using the same evidence discipline while allowing each place to retain its own structure and voice.
